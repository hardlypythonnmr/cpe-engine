Oṃ Namaḥ Śivāya.

What it is: the five-syllable mantra of Shiva (Panchakshara: na-maḥ-śi-vā-ya), with Om in front. The words "namaḥ śivāya" already appear in the Shri Rudram of the Yajurveda (Taittiriya Samhita 4.5.8). Namaḥ means "I bow"; Śivāya, "to Shiva", the auspicious one. Say it, roughly, OM nuh-MUH shi-VAA-yuh.

In the tradition: it is one of the best-known mantras of Shaivism, chanted alone on a mala or together as a bhajan. The five syllables are commonly linked to the five elements (na earth, ma water, shi fire, va air, ya space), and the tradition says that repeating them turns the mind back to Shiva, the stillness under everything.

Why a repeated sound can calm you: in a 2001 BMJ study, 23 healthy adults recited "om-mani-padme-om" and the rosary; both slowed breathing to about 6 breaths a minute and made the heart-rhythm reflexes stronger (Bernardi et al.). In a 2024 study, 34 adults chanted "om" for 12 minutes and their saliva cortisol and anxiety went down (Perry, Polito & Thompson); there was no group that didn't chant. In a pilot brain scan of 12 people, chanting "om" lowered activity in emotion areas, the amygdala among them, compared with rest; saying "ssss" did not (Kalyani et al., 2011). Small studies, short effects, a few minutes at a time: calmer, not cured. None of them tested a video like this one, a whole session, a whole bhajan or karma.

And if karma is real, this should clean a little of yours. Good karma, a quieter head, or both. No promises, no cure.

Ways to use it: sing the first rounds along and let your breath slow down; keep count with the mala on screen; put it on before meditation, yoga or a hard conversation. Listening only is fine too.
