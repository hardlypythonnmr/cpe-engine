The Maha Mantra Before the Family Group Chat Starts Planning Thursday

Cella in a kirtan courtyard, spinning in place while the family chat fills up. Forty unread, one posture. I will answer after the mantra, and not before.

Hare Kṛṣṇa Hare Kṛṣṇa, Kṛṣṇa Kṛṣṇa Hare Hare, Hare Rāma Hare Rāma, Rāma Rāma Hare Hare.

What it is: the Hare Krishna maha mantra, sixteen names from the Kali-Santarana Upanishad, sung here at a steady kirtan pace by a woman lead singer and a women's chorus, with harmonium and drum. Say it HAH-ray, KRISH-nuh, RAH-muh. (Sung in the order ISKCON chants it; the Upanishad lists Hare Rāma first.)

In the tradition: ISKCON devotees chant the mantra quietly on a string of 108 beads (japa), sixteen rounds of the string a day; sung out loud in a group, as here, it is kirtan.

Why a sound like this may calm you: in a small 2011 brain-scan pilot, 12 people chanting "om" showed lower activity in emotion areas, the right amygdala among them, than at rest; a plain "ssss" did not (Kalyani et al.). Limit: a pilot of 12 people, and mood was not measured. Two more lean the same way, with limits (Bernardi 2001: 23 healthy adults, one session; Perry 2024: 34 adults, no non-chanting group); none tested this exact mantra. Small studies, short effects: calmer, not cured.

And if karma is real, this should lighten a little of yours. If it isn't, you still let the chat wait 39 seconds.

Sing along, a word per beat, thumb off the screen. Then again.
