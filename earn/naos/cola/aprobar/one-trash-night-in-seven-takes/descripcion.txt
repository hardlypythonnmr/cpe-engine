NAOS. Choose friction.

One trash night in a shared flat, filmed seven times. The bag is full and standing by the door, and each take changes one thing: nobody says it, someone should, I'll take it out, a name on the bag, a deal, a note, and a new bag in the can. Each take ends with two lines, who has the bag and what it cost. Where there is a study it goes on the record; the rest is Cella's opinion, and nobody in the flat gets blamed. For whoever says I'll take it out before anyone asks.

What the research says (so you can check us):
Latané B, Williams K, Harkins S. (1979). Many hands make light the work: The causes and consequences of social loafing. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 37(6), 822-832. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.37.6.822
Williams KD, Harkins SG, Latané B. (1981). Identifiability as a deterrant to social loafing: Two cheering experiments. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 40(2), 303-311. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.40.2.303 (title only: the abstract is not openly available, and the video says so)
Karau SJ, Williams KD. (1993). Social loafing: A meta-analytic review and theoretical integration. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 65(4), 681-706. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.65.4.681

The voice describes each study narrowly: people asked to clap and shout put in less effort each in a group than alone, in a lab, and a trash bag is only a cousin of that; for the 1981 follow-up all we could check is its title, which says identifiability can deter social loafing; and a review of seventy-eight studies found social loafing to be robust across tasks and populations, with whether effort can be evaluated and what people expect of their co-workers among the strongest influences. The readings of a name on a bag and a deal with a day on it are Cella's, not findings.
