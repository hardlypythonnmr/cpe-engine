One hour of Om Shanti Shanti Shanti for sleep: slow, quiet rounds, soft women's voices, a whisper and a drone, around 55 beats a minute. For the first 10 minutes the word being sung lights up on screen with its meaning underneath; after that the picture slowly goes dark and the words go away. Put it on, turn the brightness down, and let it run.

Oṃ Śāntiḥ Śāntiḥ Śāntiḥ.

What it is: śānti means peace. Upanishadic peace chants (shanti mantras), such as "Saha nāv avatu" and "Pūrṇam adaḥ", end with "Om shanti shanti shanti": peace said three times. Say it, roughly, OM SHAHN-tee SHAHN-tee SHAHN-tee.

In the tradition: the three shantis are read as peace from three kinds of suffering: of your own body and mind, of other beings, and of forces beyond our control. Said at the end of a chant, it closes the door softly. Said at the end of a day, same.

Why a repeated sound can calm you: in a 2001 BMJ study, 23 healthy adults recited "om-mani-padme-om" and the rosary; both slowed breathing to about 6 breaths a minute and made the heart-rhythm reflexes stronger (Bernardi et al.). In a 2024 study, 34 adults chanted "om" for 12 minutes and their saliva cortisol and anxiety went down (Perry, Polito & Thompson); there was no group that didn't chant. In a pilot brain scan of 12 people, chanting "om" lowered activity in emotion areas, the amygdala among them, compared with rest; saying "ssss" did not (Kalyani et al., 2011). Small studies, short effects, a few minutes at a time: calmer, not cured. None of them tested a video like this one, a whole session, sleep or karma.

And if karma is real, an hour of wishing peace should clean a little of yours while you sleep. No promises, no cure. If you can't sleep for weeks, talk to a doctor; a mantra is company, not treatment.

Ways to use it: start it in bed with the brightness down; breathe out longer than you breathe in for the first few rounds; let the voice go to the background. It also works for a nap or a quiet room.
