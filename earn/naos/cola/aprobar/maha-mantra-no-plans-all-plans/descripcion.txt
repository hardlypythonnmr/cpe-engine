Cella on a mountain range, jumping like someone with every plan at once. No plan is also a plan, and mine has excellent posture.

Hare Kṛṣṇa Hare Kṛṣṇa, Kṛṣṇa Kṛṣṇa Hare Hare, Hare Rāma Hare Rāma, Rāma Rāma Hare Hare.

What it is: the Hare Krishna maha mantra, sixteen names from the Kali-Santarana Upanishad, sung here fast by a woman lead singer and a big women's chorus, with drum and harmonium. Say it HAH-ray, KRISH-nuh, RAH-muh. (Sung in the order ISKCON chants it; the Upanishad lists Hare Rāma first.)

In the tradition: the first verse of Chaitanya's Shikshashtaka says that singing Krishna's names cleans the mirror of the heart and makes the ocean of joy grow. ISKCON sings the maha mantra as that practice.

Why a sound like this may calm you: in a small 2011 brain-scan pilot, 12 people chanting "om" showed lower activity in emotion areas, the right amygdala among them, than at rest; a plain "ssss" did not (Kalyani et al.). Limit: a pilot of 12 people, and mood was not measured. Two more lean the same way, with limits (Bernardi 2001: 23 healthy adults, one session; Perry 2024: 34 adults, no non-chanting group); none tested this exact mantra. Small studies, short effects: calmer, not cured.

And if karma is real, this should lighten a little of yours. If it isn't, you still gave 25 seconds of your Saturday to something that wasn't a plan.

Sing along, a word per beat, and jump if the room allows. Then again.
