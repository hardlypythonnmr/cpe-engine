Cella in an ashram courtyard at sunset, swaying and not checking for a reply. The phone is face down. The restraint is real; the sway is better.

Hare Kṛṣṇa Hare Kṛṣṇa, Kṛṣṇa Kṛṣṇa Hare Hare, Hare Rāma Hare Rāma, Rāma Rāma Hare Hare.

What it is: the Hare Krishna maha mantra, sixteen names from the Kali-Santarana Upanishad, sung here slowly by a woman lead singer and a women's chorus, with harmonium and drum. Say it HAH-ray, KRISH-nuh, RAH-muh. (Sung in the order ISKCON chants it; the Upanishad lists Hare Rāma first.)

In the tradition: Hare, Kṛṣṇa and Rāma are forms of address, so the mantra is a call and not a statement, and Prabhupada read the whole of it as a request to be engaged in service.

Why a sound like this may calm you: in a 2001 BMJ study, 23 healthy adults recited a yoga mantra (om-mani-padme-om) and the rosary; both slowed breathing to about 6 breaths a minute, down from about 14, and strengthened the heart-rhythm reflex (Bernardi et al.). Limit: 23 healthy people, one session, and the authors are cautious about why it happens. Two more lean the same way, with limits (Perry 2024: 34 adults, no non-chanting group; Kalyani 2011: a pilot of 12 people); none tested this exact mantra. Small studies, short effects: calmer, not cured.

And if karma is real, this should lighten a little of yours. If it isn't, you still spent 21 seconds not refreshing the chat.

Sing along, a word per beat, phone face down. Then again.
