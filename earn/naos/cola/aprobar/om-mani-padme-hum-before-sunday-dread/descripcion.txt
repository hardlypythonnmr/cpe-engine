Cella in a mountain monastery, rippling slowly toward Monday. Sunday dread is a deadline with good lighting. Mine at least has the prayer flags.

Oṃ Maṇi Padme Hūṃ (sung Om Mani Peme Hung).

What it is: Om Mani Padme Hum, the six-syllable mantra of Avalokiteshvara (Chenrezig), the bodhisattva of compassion, sung here by a woman lead singer and a women's chorus at an even study pace. Tibetans say it Om Mani Peme Hung, and that's how it is sung here.

In the tradition: the Dalai Lama explains that Om stands for the practitioner's impure body, speech and mind and also for the pure body, speech and mind of a Buddha, and the practice is the path from the first to the second.

Why a sound like this may calm you: in a 2001 BMJ study, 23 healthy adults recited a yoga mantra (om-mani-padme-om) and the rosary; both slowed breathing to about 6 breaths a minute, down from about 14, and strengthened the heart-rhythm reflex (Bernardi et al.). Limit: 23 healthy people, one session, and the authors are cautious about why it happens. Two more lean the same way, with limits (Perry 2024: 34 adults, no non-chanting group; Kalyani 2011: a pilot of 12 people). Small studies, short effects: calmer, not cured.

And if karma is real, this should clean a little of yours. If it isn't, you still gave Sunday evening 34 seconds that Monday didn't get.

Chant it along, one syllable to a wave. Six rounds, then once more.
